INSIDE THE CHAMBER

Quiet architecture.
Exacting questions.

I

A room that holds the dark

An expanded courtyard is enclosed by a deep stone hall, a four-bay shaded gallery and detailed neighbouring buildings. Beside it, the shuttered chamber receives daylight through a small opening and projects the same courtyard upside down.

II

A place to measure

A generously spaced optical bench sits beside the light path, with clear access along both sides. The surrounding mirror, sighting and astronomy stations are enlarged for clearer inspection, with usable table heights and open routes between them.

III

A place to think

The geometry lectern has its own corner. Instrument shelves and book storage occupy the perimeter, leaving open floor between observation, drafting and storage areas.

Inspired by the experiments. The architecture, room layout, furnishings and courtyard are artistic interpretations. The source notes distinguish the historical principles from the reconstructed setting.

EVIDENCE & INTERPRETATION

A chamber for
the study of light.

Al-bayt al-muẓlim means “the dark room.” Ibn al-Haytham described dark-chamber experiments involving light admitted through small openings. It is a term for an experimental enclosure; the exact private sanctuary pictured here is not established by the sources.

In the Optics, distinct candle flames produce separate patches of light. His later work on eclipses considers solar-crescent imaging. The courtyard projection here is a modern explanatory demonstration of the pinhole principle.

What is reconstructed

The masonry, pointed arches, shutter, brass plate, canvas, bench, lectern, shelving and their arrangement follow your artistic brief. Glass and geometric tools connect the room to his optical studies. The hanging astrolabes provide period context; their personal ownership is not documented here.

Mirrors, observation & time

The seven polished-iron mirror families follow Ibn al-Haytham’s reflection studies. The lensless copper sighting tube, meridian instrument and gnomon draw on his meridian treatises. The water clock follows a manuscript-based reconstruction with a sinking cylinder and 24-division dial. Mounts, dimensions and placement are artistic interpretations.

The station controls provide indoor teaching experiments. Mirror rays follow the selected surface and the law of reflection; complete reflected images are not rendered. Sighting-tube alignment and quadrant angles are geometric demonstrations. The gnomon uses a controlled demonstration light, while actual astronomical observations require outdoor sky or sunlight. Clock motion is illustrative, without a hydraulic flow simulation.

How to read the light

The courtyard image is rendered from the position of the pinhole, then inverted on the screen. A larger opening admits more light and softens the image. Framing, magnification and aperture blur are illustrative; this qualitative model does not calculate diffraction or use calibrated physical dimensions. Visible lines are a teaching overlay; real light shafts need scattering particles such as dust. “Inspection light” adds visibility for exploring the artwork.
